Anyone who lives in Latin America knows that prices go up, currencies fall, and salaries lose value at a speed that respects neither calendars nor payrolls. Crises become the backdrop of everyday life and, with time, dangerously, a habit (Argentina, Venezuela, Bolivia).

In this context, **financial education is not a privilege, it is a basic need**. Not only to preserve the value of money, but to preserve the ability to choose. Because when crises come, and they always come, there is an abysmal difference between those who face them with tools and those who face them with fear.

Financial education doesn't guarantee wealth, but it offers perspective. It lets you understand that, even in a hostile environment, there are possible moves: decisions that minimize the damage, strategies that protect what is yours, even opportunities that arise precisely **because the system is in crisis**.

But financial education, in its most transformative version, also reaches deeper dimensions: our beliefs about money, the family stories we were told, the place we take within our household economy, our self-esteem.

Now Bri, what is #Season2? Deep down, a political, emotional and collective answer to a system that never prepared us to take control of our finances, and even less of our inner narratives.

Yesterday this new season began, completely free, with the workshop **“6 steps to financial freedom”** by Cecilia Jordan Yanez. It was a conversation about something deeper than money: a reflection on the roots of our economic decisions. Which family stories tie us to a fear of money? Which patterns do we repeat without noticing? What stops us from talking about saving, investing and growth without feeling guilt or shame? And so, a bit of “manifestation”, which apparently works for so many people... (Not for me, personally.) But seeing around 200 people signed up and open to this space, it is definitely worth sharing. From a simple but deeply articulated approach, Cecilia proposed looking inward before moving a single figure: diagnose, unlearn, give new meaning. Because no strategy works if we still believe we don't deserve stability.

She Knows, as a platform, is not promising magic formulas. It is doing something far more valuable: it is opening spaces where women can **name their worries without fear, acquire tools without being judged, and start building something more solid than good luck: judgment and awareness.**

Season 2 presents itself as a cultural response to a structural need. It offers training, yes. But it also offers legitimacy: the legitimacy of saying that **women can not only learn about money, they can decide about it.**

And it does so for free. At a time when knowledge is traded as a commodity and self-help has become a business, She Knows chooses to share what it knows with generosity and with rigor. A decision that, in the middle of crisis and uncertainty, **is deeply revolutionary**.

In a country, and in a region, where the precarious has become permanent, having access to practical, accessible and ethical knowledge can make the difference between repeating the cycle and breaking it. And perhaps that is why **financial education is not only a tool for defense, but also for transformation**. Because if crises are cyclical, so can opportunities be.
