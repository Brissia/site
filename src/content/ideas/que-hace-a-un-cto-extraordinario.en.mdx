---

Not every startup is born with a CTO among its founders, but it should be! That is how one of my clients came to ask me: "What should I evaluate when bringing a CTO onto the team?"

So I got in touch with the people I look up to in technology, the professionals I respect most in this field, to ask them a simple question with quite a lot behind it.

**What makes a CTO extraordinary? And what makes one mediocre.**

CTOs in Bolivia, Europe and the US, bank executives in Bolivia and Central America, and senior developers at Big Tech companies shared their perspectives with me, so here is the most relevant of it.

---

## What makes a CTO extraordinary

### 1. They build people, not just systems

This was the common denominator in the answers. Extraordinary CTOs understand something that sounds basic but is fundamental!

👉 **If you don't know how to develop people, you can't scale technology.**

[🇧🇴 Mario-César Señoranis Ayala](https://www.linkedin.com/in/mariocesar) summed it up quite well:

> *“An extraordinary CTO makes someone come in as a zero and leave as a hundred.”*

The mediocre CTO looks for “ready-made stars”. The extraordinary one **develops talent and multiplies it**.

---

### 2. They have a “business technologist” mindset, not a stack evangelist's

As [Beto Saavedra](https://www.linkedin.com/in/betosaavedra) said, CTOs shouldn't fall into the trap of technological adrenaline.

> *“An extraordinary CTO is a business strategist first, a technologist second.”*

They know when to experiment and when not to. When to bet on the hype and when to protect the roadmap. They don't fall in love with the new toy: they fall in love with the impact.

---

### 3. They know how to delegate, and they do it without fear

This point comes straight from [Cecilia Uriona](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ceciuriona)

> *“The best CTOs I have known have learned to delegate.”*

The inability to let go of the keyboard is, literally, one of the biggest bottlenecks in the ecosystem. The extraordinary CTO stops “trying things out” and starts **directing architectures, people and decisions**.

---

### 4. They bring together business, architecture, risk and culture

The extraordinary ones don't only know the stack; they know:

- regulation,
- infrastructure,
- product design,
- cybersecurity,
- and the psychology of the team.

As [Fabiola Cerveró](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ACoAAASLpC8BcNDhGt92I4zRL3-YhbGpbU_vYOg) says, they are able to:

> *“Align technology with the company's goals, manage teams and make things happen.”*

They are the bridge between the what, the how and the when.

---

### 5. They make uncomfortable technical decisions, even under pressure from the C-suite

Here comes the surgical clarity of [Mihai Anisiu](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ACoAABmmlCABgYmPw1uDx8Xv8pUMjBHFLmPdQqk) **(Meta)**:

> *“An extraordinary CTO pushes for the right technologies, not the fashionable ones, and doesn't give in to pressures that compromise the future.”*

On a continent where many CTOs still follow orders instead of leading technology, this is a vital distinction.

---

### 6. They make the complex simple

[Diego Fuentes Chavez](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ACoAAAX8Y5cBwapDWWO3jLXEAvOirafowbkkSMc) defined it precisely:

> *“An extraordinary CTO simplifies processes so the business understands and moves forward.”*

They don't make the business move at their technical pace: **they translate technology to the pace of the business**.

---

## OK OK Bri! But what makes a CTO mediocre

Here comes the uncomfortable part. Please don't take it personally!

Mediocre CTOs are not bad technologists. The problem is that **they are excellent technologists trapped in their own comfort zone**.

---

### 1. They confuse engineering with leadership

They believe their value lies in showing off their endless machine hours.

The mediocre ones, as Mario César said:

- hire expensively,
- spend without judgment,
- don't develop talent,
- and leave a team the same as, or worse than, when they arrived.

---

### 2. They are slaves to the stack they know

Cecilia explained it brilliantly:

> *“The CTO's limit becomes the organization's limit.”*

If the CTO doesn't stay current, neither does the company. If the CTO avoids risk, the company grows old.

---

### 3. They don't approach planning strategically

Here Mihai was blunt:

> *"They accept deadlines, solutions or technologies just to please, even when they are technically unviable or harmful in the long run."*

With no deep analysis to design a roadmap aligned with the company's strategy.

---

### 4. The real challenge is communicating clearly

[Nicolas DEPOILLY](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ACoAAA109s4BN9M3LBi5nJ2cTcTqrpyWn9Ab7iQ) touches on something few people say so honestly:

> *“If a CTO only knows how to talk to the technology people and can't translate it for every stakeholder, they bring no value to the C-suite.”*

An extraordinary one lives in a permanent state of learning, updating their judgment and adapting their decisions to the context of the business. And they are experts at translating complexity into clarity.

---

### 5. They confuse “complying with regulation” with blocking innovation

In the words of Diego Fuentes:

> *“The mediocre CTO puts the rule first so strictly that it blocks the opportunity to innovate.”*

In the Latin American regulatory context, this mistake costs double.

---

### 6. They operate without a systems mindset: they live putting out fires

This is perhaps the clearest, and most dangerous, symptom of a mediocre CTO.

As [Fabian Benavente](https://www.linkedin.com/in/fabian-benavente) pointed out, in serious industries there is no such thing as a CTO who improvises:

> *“If you don't have an FMEA mindset, you will spend your life putting out fires instead of creating systems so those problems don't happen.”*

A mediocre CTO **reacts**. An extraordinary CTO **designs**.

If this exercise reveals anything, it is that the extraordinary CTO is not a “support” role, but a strategic role that defines the destiny of the organization. Senior leadership has to understand that technology is no longer a functional area: it is a business thesis. A mediocre CTO compromises the company's architecture, culture and speed.

The question is not which technology your company will adopt, but who you are trusting with the responsibility of deciding it.
