That is metacognition.

Without that layer of awareness, thinking turns automatic, reactive, indulgent with its own biases. It may sound sophisticated, but it is running on autopilot.

## OK Bri, but what is metacognition, really?

It is not naive introspection, and it is not a "personal development" hack.

Metacognition is the ability to **observe, monitor and regulate our own mental processes** while they are happening. It means noticing:

- what we know and what we only think we know,
- when a mental strategy has stopped working,
- the moment an emotion starts to hijack our judgment,
- and how to change course before we land on fragile conclusions.

People with well-developed metacognition don't necessarily think faster. **They think with more control, more pause and more awareness.** And that changes everything.

The good news: metacognition is not an innate talent. **It can be trained.**

## These are the 3 exercises that have worked best for me

They are not comfortable. They work precisely because they make you uncomfortable.

---

## 1. The cognitive friction journal

Don't record what you thought. Record **where you hesitated**.

After a meaningful decision, a heated discussion or a strong opinion, write down:

- At what point did I feel absolutely certain?
- What information did I dismiss too quickly?
- Which assumptions did I take as valid without checking them?

This exercise trains you to catch **mental automatisms** and blind spots. Thinking better starts with identifying **where we stopped thinking** and started reacting.

---

## 2. The scalpel question: What would make me change my mind?

Before you defend an idea, ask yourself this, no anesthesia:

> What concrete evidence would invalidate this conclusion?

If you can't find any, you are not reasoning: you are protecting a belief.

This exercise forces you to put ideas under intellectual stress. A critical mind doesn't look to confirm itself; **it looks to be tested**. That is where judgment really gets refined.

---

## 3. The post-decision audit

One week after an important decision, review it honestly:

- Which mental strategy did I use?
- What worked and what didn't?
- What would I do differently if I faced the same situation today?

This exercise turns experience into real learning. Without this review, even good decisions go to waste. With it, thinking evolves instead of going around in circles.
